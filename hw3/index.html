Overview

In this homework, we implemented a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) accelerated path tracer, capable of rendering Collada DAE scenes with diffuse BSDF shading. For Part 1, our approach to determining ray intersections was similar to that of HW 1 and 2. We used our understanding of barycentric coordinates and interpolating normals to determine ray-surface intersections.

For Part 2, we implemented the BVH, splitting along the longest edge and at the average of the centroids of primitives, making sure to split arbitrarily if all primitives end up on one side to avoid an infinite recursion.

In Part 3, we implemented the direct lighting functions. In order to get one bounce lighting (camera->object->light), we send out shadow rays at the intersection point of each camera ray with the scene, and accumulate these to form an average color for the pixel at the intersection point.

Part 4 takes this one step further, where instead of just casting shadow rays, we cast an additional ray at the intersection point in a direction based on the surface BSDF, and add up the indirect light contributions from the bounces of this ray off other objects.

Part 5 allows us to speed up our renders by concentrating samples where they're needed most, rather than using a uniform sampling rate over the entire image.


Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection (20 Points)

Walk through the ray generation and primitive intersection parts of the rendering pipeline.

Ray Generation:

To generate a camera ray that passes through the sensor, we first transform the x and y coordinates of the image to the camera space. We do this by mapping (1,1) in image space to (tan(hFOV/2), tan(vFOV/2), -1), and (0,0) to (-tan(hFOV/2), -tan(vFOV/2), -1). Then, we determine the direction to that point in camera space and transform that to world space. We finally create a ray starting from the current position of the camera, with the direction in world space we computed.

Primitive Intersection:

The naive approach to rendering a scene is to simply cast a ray and check, for each object in the scene, whether the ray intersects. We generate ns_aa random rays for each pixel, and sample the radiance along each ray. In this part, this radiance is simply a color corresponding to the surface normal of the surface the ray intersects.

The details of the intersection check vary with the primitive. We implemented intersection checking for triangles, and additionaly spheres. This allows us to render spheres without wasting many triangles to achieve a smooth look. For the sphere, we can simply solve a quadratic equation for the t within tmin and tmax that intersects the sphere.


Explain the triangle intersection algorithm you implemented in your own words.

To determine whether a ray intersects a triangle, we have to determine whether the ray is parallel to the triangle, and if not, determine the position of the ray's intersection with the plane defined by the triangle. Then, we can use barycentric coordinates to determine if this point is inside or outside the triangle.

By the Möller–Trumbore algorithm, we decompose this problem into solving a matrix equation for t, the point on the ray the intersection occurs, and u, and v, the point on the plane relative to two edges of the triangle.

\[ \begin{bmatrix} t \\ u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\vec{S_1}\cdot\vec{E_1}} \begin{bmatrix} \vec{S_2} \cdot \vec{E_2} \\ \vec{S_1} \cdot \vec{S} \\ \vec{S_2} \cdot \vec{D} \end{bmatrix} \]

where $\vec{O}$ and $\vec{D}$ represent the origin and direction of the ray, respectively, and $\vec{P_1}$, $\vec{P_2}$, $\vec{P_3}$, represent the points of the triangle, and

\[ \vec{E_1} = \vec{P_1}-\vec{P_0} \] \[ \vec{E_2} = \vec{P_2}-\vec{P_0} \] \[ \vec{S} = \vec{O}-\vec{P_0} \] \[ \vec{S_1} = \vec{D}-\vec{E_2} \] \[ \vec{S_2} = \vec{S}-\vec{E_1} \]

We can then simply compute these values and test if u or v are less than 1, and if their sum is less than 0. We also test that t is within tmin and tmax

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy (20 Points)

Walk through your BVH construction algorithm. Explain the heuristic you chose for picking the splitting point.

To construct the BVH, we recursively split the scene into bounding boxes, with the axis of each split being the longest axis of the box. This ensures the child nodes will have less overlap, reducing the amount of unnecessary intersection checks. We then determine the average of the centroids of the primitives in the box, and split along this point.

In the case that we split the primitives, and all end up one one side, we simply split in the middle of the list of primitives. This avoids an infinite recursion which places the same few primitives in the same side over and over.

Then, we recurse, splitting both sides of the box in the same way until the number of primitives in each box is under a certain threshold value.

Part 3: Direct Illumination (20 Points)

Walk through both implementations of the direct lighting function.

Both importance and uniform lighting functions follow the same basic principle. We cast out at most ns_area_light shadow rays from the point of intersection. If a given ray intersects a light, we add it's contribution to the total reflectance using the reflectance equation, and then dividing at the end by the number of samples to obtain an average reflectance.

For the uniform lighting function, the direction of the shadow ray is chosen uniformly from the hemisphere, which means that the ray has a chance to miss the light entirely. In the importance lighting function, on the other hand, the direction of the shadow ray is chosen with the direction to the light source in mind. The probability of casting a ray in the direction of the light source is weighted more, as only these contribute data to the final result. In order for our estimate to remain unbiased, we must divide by the probability distribution function for this sampling method.

Part 4: Global Illumination (20 Points)

Walk through your implementation of the indirect lighting function.

The indirect lighting function works similarly to the direct versions. We take in a ray and an intersection point, and then send out a new ray to determine how bright the pixel at the intersection point is. Instead of sending a ray to the light, however, we send a ray in a sample direction and recursively track the bounces for a given maximum ray depth.

We use the BSDF of the surface we intersect to determine the direction of the bounce ray. Then, we check for intersections, and call the indirect lighting function on this ray as well. The final result is the reflectance coefficients (including the probability density function of the sample direction) multiplied with the result of the recursion, plus the direct lighting contribution at each step.

With the Russian Roulette implementation, we stop the recursion at every bounce with a probability of 0.4, or at a max recursion depth defined by max_ray_depth The final return is the direct lighting of the last bounce.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling (20 Points)

Explain adaptive sampling. Walk through your implementation of the adaptive sampling.

Adaptive sampling utilizes the fact that not all areas in the scene need the same amount of samples to converge to a non-noisy result. We can calculate the convergence of a pixel by computing the standard error of the mean of the samples illuminance. We use illuminance because it better represents how noisy the image will actually appear over using just the norm of the sample reflectance. The convergence is actually the SEM times a coefficient of 1.96, which represents our confidence interval of 95%.

If the convergence is below the product of a threshold and the mean illuminance (ie. we are 95% confident the true illuminance of the pixel is between $\mu+I$ and $\mu-I$). If this condition is met, we can stop sampling, and divide our total reflectance by the actual number of samples we performed.

Our implementation of the adaptive sampling checks the convergence every samplesPerBatch samples, so as not to perform this costly check for every sample. The rest of our implementation follows from the above discussion. Every samplesPerBatch camera samples, we compute the mean, and standard deviation of the pixel illuminance, compute I, and check the threshold condition. If the condition is met, we stop sampling and report the average over the true number of samples as the color for that pixel.
